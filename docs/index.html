The way we share out protection favours the richer side of town

Most flood works are approved with a simple test: does the project save more property value than it costs? Zachary Liscow of Yale gives an example. Two levees cost $10 million each. Levee A protects 50 homes worth $210,000. Levee B protects 100 homes worth $90,000. Counted in property value, A saves $10.5 million and passes. B saves $9 million and fails, though it protects twice as many homes.

Economists have a fix called equity weights. A dollar saved counts for more when the household that saves it has less. How much more is set by one number, written η. At zero, every dollar counts the same. Move the slider and watch what happens.

How the weights are computed

Each dollar saved by a household is multiplied by (average income ÷ household income)η. With income following home value, the average across the 150 homes is $130,000, so a home in A is weighted (130/210)η and a home in B (130/90)η. B overtakes A at η ≈ 0.18 and passes the $10 million cost at η ≈ 0.29.

THRIVE builds this idea into the allocation from the start. The choice of η is political, so THRIVE never fixes it. It shows an elected council what each value would mean and leaves the decision to them.

Who recovered first? It depends on how you ask

Satellites can see a neighbourhood come back to life: lights return at night, and fields turn green again. To compare areas, you have to choose a threshold for "recovered". The two curves below are invented, but the problem is real. A 2026 study in Texas found that its answer to "do poorer areas recover more slowly?" flipped when the definition changed.
