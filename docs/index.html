Who is protected first, and who recovers?

When the money for flood protection runs out, someone decides which neighbourhood comes first. After the water goes down, some areas are back within weeks and others wait for months. THRIVE is being built to make those decisions fair, and to show that they are.

Protecting: who gets the levee?

Many benefit-cost tests for flood protection count mostly the property value a project protects. Zachary Liscow of Yale gives an example, shown here in euros. Two levees cost €10 million each. Levee A protects 50 homes worth €210,000. Levee B protects 100 homes worth €90,000. Counted in property value, A saves €10.5 million and passes, while B saves €9 million and fails. B protects twice as many homes.

Economists have a fix called equity weights. A euro saved counts for more when the household that saves it has less, and one number, η, sets how much more. Drag it and watch the decision change.

How the weights are computed

Each euro saved by a household is multiplied by (average income ÷ household income)η. With income following home value, the average across the 150 homes is €130,000, so a home in A is weighted (130/210)η and a home in B (130/90)η. B overtakes A at η ≈ 0.18 and passes the €10 million cost at η ≈ 0.29.

THRIVE builds this idea into the allocation from the start. Choosing η is a political decision, so THRIVE never fixes it. It shows an elected council what each value would mean and leaves the choice to them.

Thessaly: who lived where the water of Daniel reached?

Phase 1 lays the flood maps of Storm Daniel, made by the Copernicus Emergency Management Service from satellite images in September 2023, over the 2021 census on Eurostat's grid of 1 km squares. The grid gives the residents of every square and how many of them are 65 or over, and its totals match the census of ELSTAT to within 0.2% in every regional unit.

What counts as flooded is itself a choice. Flood traces mark ground the water had already left when the image was taken. So THRIVE answers the same question three times. Pick a definition.

In Karditsa and Trikala the ratio is above 1 (1.07 to 1.19), but the intervals run from 0.89 to 1.30, so the pattern is not firm enough to report. In Larisa the ratio is below 1 (0.79 to 0.87), but the intervals run from 0.53 to 1.13, so the pattern is not firm enough to report. They show how much a ratio depends on which places the water happened to reach.

Magnesia shows almost no one in the flood (99 to 123 residents, depending on the map). That says little about Volos and the villages of Pelion: their floods were flash floods in streets and streams that drained within hours, and satellites passing later rarely see them. Being in the flood is also not the same as being harmed, and open data cannot tell the two apart.

Thriving: who recovered first?

Satellites can see a neighbourhood come back to life: lights return at night, and fields turn green again. To compare areas you have to choose what counts as recovered. The two curves below are invented, but the problem is real. In Thessaly after Storm Daniel, which group of flooded land recovered first changed with the threshold and with the number of ten-day periods in a row the land had to stay above it, so THRIVE does not report it as a finding (Phase 2). Thessaly: did the flooded land come back?

The two curves above were made up. These were measured. THRIVE used 2,511 Sentinel-2 images of the Thessalian plain, taken between 2019 and the end of 2024, and worked out how green each flooded square kilometre was in every ten-day period. A square is compared with the same weeks of 2019 to 2021 in that same square, and then with unflooded land 3 to 15 km away, so a dry summer across the whole plain does not pass for flood damage. In case the land nearest the flood was hurt too, every comparison was repeated with unflooded land 5 to 20 and 10 to 30 km away.

Some of the curves look wrong at first. In Karditsa and Trikala the flooded land was close to normal by late October 2023, only weeks after the water, and greener than normal at times during the winter. A year on, from late September to late October 2024, the flooded land as a whole was 9 to 24% greener than in a normal autumn. The cotton harvest goes some way to explain this. Daniel struck a few weeks before it, and cotton was the crop hit hardest: more than 282 km² of it went under water, about 30% of the cotton of central Greece. In a normal year cotton is picked from late September, most of it by early November, so in a normal autumn the fields are picked and left bare, and a field that was never harvested, or that later grew weeds or another crop, can look greener than normal while yielding nothing. Many flooded fields were never harvested, and in July 2024 reporters still found unharvested crops in mud-filled fields near Larisa. Greenness cannot tell a ruined crop from weeds, so the findings below rest on the spring and summer of 2024, when the fields are green in a normal year too.

Show the numbers by season, with 95% intervals

Two of the three maps give almost the same curves, and that is not a mistake. Of the 827 squares under standing water, 826 are also inside the Copernicus maximum, which adds 77 more, with 55 residents in all; 35 of them lie in Magnesia, with 0 residents. Then 62 squares of the Copernicus map drop out as land that is not green in a normal year. The two maps end up measuring almost the same fields, so their curves never differ by more than about one point.

In the summer of 2024, almost a year after Daniel, the flooded land was still 13 to 15 % less green than normal under every flood map and every control area, with 95 % intervals running from 9 to 23 %. Larisa's shortfall against Karditsa and Trikala was 19 to 28 points in the spring, and every interval stays below zero, and 9 to 12 in the summer, but not every interval stays on one side of zero, so the gap is not firm under every map and control area. Part of that gap is a new lake: the basin of the former Lake Karla filled with water in September 2023 and stayed flooded well into 2024. Leaving out Larisa's squares within 15 km of the old lake's centre, the gap is 5 points in the spring and −1 to 1 in the summer, and every interval includes zero, so no difference shows. Between flooded squares with more and with fewer residents aged 65 and over no difference shows. In the summer the two groups differ by 0 to 1 points, but each group holds only 27 to 78 squares, so the intervals run from about 7 points less to 9 points more. A modest gap either way cannot be ruled out. Every interval resamples the squares in blocks of 10 by 10 km, because neighbouring squares flood and recover together.

In Magnesia the flooded squares were still mostly water in the spring of 2024, and the summer gave too few clear images for a figure. Greenness also has its limits as a measure. It shows fields coming back, not households: a farm can be green again while the family that works it is still waiting for help.

Greece: what does a wildfire cost Tourism?

In November 2026 the Hellenic Parliamentary Budget Office published the first estimate of what climate extremes cost the regions of Greece. Its clearest result concerns wildfires: Tourism-related services (trade, transport, accommodation and food) grow more slowly after a fire, mostly in the season after it, when bookings fall. Before building anything on that result, THRIVE rebuilt it from open data alone: every burnt area mapped by the European Forest Fire Information System since 2016, matched to the 52 regions it crossed, and the regional accounts of Eurostat. The run stops if any number differs from the Note.

The replication holds exactly. The Rhodes fire of 2023 burnt 18,687 hectares, 6.9% of the region, as in the Note, and every one of the 24 coefficients matches it to the second decimal. For Tourism-related services the change is −0.21 points in the fire year and −0.54 points in the year after, −0.80 points over three years.

The Note ends in 2023, when the regional accounts end, so it sees the fires of Rhodes and Evros only in their fire year. Eurostat counts the nights spent at tourist accommodation up to 2025, so THRIVE asked the same question of nights, under four checks fixed in advance.

All 8 estimates point the same way, from −3.48 to −0.54, but none is significant (the smallest p is 0.12), so by THRIVE's own rule no finding is reported. Fewer nights after a fire cannot be ruled out, but on these data the loss the Note finds in value added does not show clearly as a loss of nights. Fewer nights per guest, lower prices or lower spending per night would each fit it; telling them apart needs data that are not yet open.

Attica: what do street trees do in a heatwave?

Phase 4 runs BUARO, the open software that implements the Bayesian urban adaptation and resilience model of the book AI for Planetary Resilience, on the seven regions of Attica. It reads the weekly deaths of Eurostat for each region, daily temperature from ERA5-Land of Copernicus, the 2021 census grid, the Urban Atlas and the Street Tree Layer 2021 of Copernicus, and every clear Summer image of Landsat 8 and 9 since 2014, 246 in all.

Across Attica, each 1°C above the local heat threshold raises weekly deaths by about 5.4% (95% interval 4.4% to 6.4%), from 3.6% in Central Athens to 6.1% in Western Athens. Each Summer from 2023 is forecast only with data up to 31 May, and knowing the heat improved the forecast in 2 of the 3 Summers tested.

The model learned from the Summers of 2014 to 2022 and was then tested on 2023 to 2026, which it had never seen. Each 10 percentage points more street tree cover lowers the surface temperature by 0.23°C (0.20 to 0.27), and each unseen Summer confirms it on its own. Surface temperature is not air temperature: across European cities, trees cool the daytime air many times less than the surface (Du et al., Environmental Research Letters, 2024), so THRIVE reports the cooling of the surface and does not turn it into deaths avoided.

At 3°C of global warming, the same Summers would bring about 2.3 times today's heat-related deaths in Attica, unless vulnerability falls. A trend suggesting that Attica is adapting disappears when the Summer of 2025 is left out, so it is not reported as a finding.
