Who is protected first, and who recovers?

When the money for flood protection runs out, someone decides which neighbourhood comes first. After the water goes down, some areas are back within weeks and others wait for months. THRIVE is being built to make those decisions fair, and to show that they are.

Protecting: who gets the levee?

In the United States, flood-mitigation projects funded by FEMA must pass a benefit-cost test, and the benefits it counts are mostly the property value a project protects. Zachary Liscow of Yale gives an example. Two levees cost $10 million each. Levee A protects 50 homes worth $210,000. Levee B protects 100 homes worth $90,000. Counted in property value, A saves $10.5 million and passes, while B saves $9 million and fails. B protects twice as many homes.

Economists have a fix called equity weights. A dollar saved counts for more when the household that saves it has less, and one number, η, sets how much more. Drag it and watch the decision change.

How the weights are computed

Each dollar saved by a household is multiplied by (average income ÷ household income)η. With income following home value, the average across the 150 homes is $130,000, so a home in A is weighted (130/210)η and a home in B (130/90)η. B overtakes A at η ≈ 0.18 and passes the $10 million cost at η ≈ 0.29.

THRIVE builds this idea into the allocation from the start. Choosing η is a political decision, so THRIVE never fixes it. It shows an elected council what each value would mean and leaves the choice to them.

First results: who got the flood money in the United States?

Phase A is the first time THRIVE runs on real data. It adds up the flood-related FEMA hazard-mitigation grants since 2000, $13.72 billion placed in 3,140 counties, and divides each county's money by its expected yearly flood loss from the National Risk Index. The counties are then split into five equal groups by social vulnerability.

Thessaly: who lived where the water of Daniel reached?

Phase B lays the flood maps of Storm Daniel, made by the Copernicus Emergency Management Service from satellite images in September 2023, over the 2021 census on Eurostat's grid of 1 km squares. The grid gives the residents of every square and how many of them are 65 or over, and its totals match the census of ELSTAT to within 0.2% in every regional unit.

What counts as flooded is itself a choice. Flood traces mark ground the water had already left when the image was taken. So THRIVE answers the same question three times. Pick a definition.

Larisa is the one clear case. Under every flood map, older people there were less likely than their neighbours to live where the water reached, and the 95% interval stays below 1 each time. In Karditsa and Trikala the ratio is above 1 under every map, but with flood traces counted its interval comes down to 0.99, so that pattern is not firm enough to report. For Thessaly as a whole the answer flips with the map. The intervals come from resampling the 1 km squares a thousand times: they show how much a ratio depends on which squares the water happened to reach.

Magnesia shows almost no one in the flood (99 residents). That says little about Volos and the villages of Pelion: their floods were flash floods in streets and streams that drained within hours, and satellites passing later rarely see them. Being in the flood is also not the same as being harmed, and open data cannot tell the two apart.

Thriving: who recovered first?

Satellites can see a neighbourhood come back to life: lights return at night, and fields turn green again. To compare areas you have to choose what counts as recovered. Thessaly: did the flooded land come back?

The two curves above were made up. These were measured. THRIVE used 2,511 Sentinel-2 images of the Thessalian plain, taken between 2019 and the end of 2024, and worked out how green each flooded square kilometre was in every ten-day period. A square is compared with the same weeks of 2019 to 2021 in that same square, and then with unflooded land 3 to 15 km away, so a dry summer across the whole plain does not pass for flood damage. In case the land nearest the flood was hurt too, every comparison was repeated with unflooded land 5 to 20 and 10 to 30 km away.

One result looked wrong at first. In October 2023, a month after the storm, much of the flooded land was greener than in a normal October. The timing helps explain it. Daniel struck a few weeks before the cotton harvest, and cotton was the crop hit hardest: more than 282 km² of it went under water, about 30% of the cotton of central Greece. In a normal year those fields are picked and bare by October. Many flooded fields were never harvested, and in July 2024 reporters still found unharvested crops in mud-filled fields near Larisa. Greenness cannot tell a ruined crop from weeds, so the findings below rest on the next growing season.

Show the numbers by season, with 95% intervals

Two of the three maps give almost the same curves, and that is not a mistake. Of the 827 squares under standing water, 826 are also inside the Copernicus maximum, which adds 77 more: 35 of them in Magnesia, with 55 residents between all 77. Then 62 squares of the Copernicus map drop out as land that is not green in a normal year. The two maps end up measuring almost the same fields, so their curves never differ by more than about one point.

Three things hold under every flood map and every control area. A year after Daniel, in the summer of 2024, the flooded land was still 13 to 15% less green than normal, with 95% intervals running from 12 to 17%. Larisa fared worse than Karditsa and Trikala, by 19 to 28 points in the spring and 9 to 12 in the summer, and none of the nine intervals comes near zero. Around villages with more older residents, no difference shows: in the summer that land was about 1 point greener than the rest. The groups are small, though, and the intervals run from about 5 points less to 7 points more, so a modest gap either way cannot be ruled out.

In Magnesia the flooded squares were still mostly water in the spring of 2024, and the summer gave too few clear images for a figure. Greenness also has its limits as a measure. It shows fields coming back, not households: a farm can be green again while the family that works it is still waiting for help.
